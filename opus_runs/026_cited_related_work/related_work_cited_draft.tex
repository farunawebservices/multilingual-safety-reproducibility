\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Steering and activation engineering}

Activation-level control of language models has developed from a small set of
recipes: extracting latent steering vectors by optimisation
\citep{subramani2022extracting}, adding contrastive activation differences at
inference time \citep{turner2023activation,rimsky2024steering}, intervening on
probe-identified directions to change truthfulness behaviour
\citep{li2023inference}, and reading and controlling higher-level concepts
through representation engineering \citep{zou2023representation}. Earlier
decoding-time controlled-generation methods target the same behavioural surface
without touching internal activations \citep{dathathri2020plug,liu2021dexperts}.
A parallel line treats weight- and task-space edits as the control surface
\citep{ilharco2023task}, and a third identifies interpretable directions with
sparse autoencoders
\citep{cunningham2023sparse,bricken2023monosemanticity,templeton2024scaling},
now at scale and with open feature libraries
\citep{gao2025scaling,lieberum2024gemmascope}. Most directly relevant to safety,
\citet{arditi2024refusal} report that refusal in several open-weight chat models
is well described by a single mediating direction, and
\citet{marks2024geometry} and \citet{vonrutte2024language} study the geometry of
related concept directions; the linear-representation framing that underwrites
such claims is examined by \citet{park2024linear}, and steering has also been
applied to non-safety behaviours such as instruction following
\citep{stolfo2025improving}.

Three features of this literature bound its evidential reach, and they define
the space in which the present paper sits. First, evaluation is overwhelmingly
English and single-turn: steering effects are measured on chat completions or
instruction prompts \citep{turner2023activation,rimsky2024steering}, not on
decisions taken inside a running system. Second, steering vectors generalise
unevenly across prompt distributions and models; \citet{tan2024analysing} show
that reliability varies substantially by behaviour and setting, which makes
single-model demonstrations weak evidence for the technique as such. Third,
mechanistic and behavioural claims are often reported together even when only
the latter is measured, and subspace-level interventions can appear causally
implicated when they are not \citep{makelov2024illusion}. We deliberately stay
on the behavioural side. This paper reports prompt-matched, within-model
condition contrasts, and makes \emph{no} mechanistic or localisation claim: it
does not replicate, confirm, or extend the refusal-direction results above
\citep{arditi2024refusal}, the historical steering direction used in the
archived Phase~2 material is not available in our evidence base, and no matched
random-vector control on the same prompts and outcome definition exists anywhere
in it. Generic perturbation is therefore neither identified nor ruled out, and
vector specificity is not established here. What is new relative to this
literature is scope rather than mechanism: a multilingual, multi-model,
agentic-pipeline evaluation of steering applied at a specific pipeline
\emph{role}, with per-model correction families and explicit coverage
accounting.

\subsection{Agentic systems and tool use}

Agentic LLM systems decompose a task across roles and tool calls: interleaved
reasoning and acting \citep{yao2023react}, tool invocation
\citep{schick2023toolformer,qin2024toolllm}, self-critique and revision
\citep{madaan2023selfrefine,shinn2023reflexion,saunders2022selfcritiquing},
model-written critiques as an alignment mechanism \citep{bai2022constitutional},
multi-agent debate \citep{du2024improving,khan2024debating}, and orchestration
frameworks with explicit role assignment
\citep{park2023generative,wu2023autogen,hong2024metagpt}. Safety evaluation for
such systems has grown correspondingly: sandboxed risk elicitation
\citep{ruan2024toolemu}, prompt-injection and tool-abuse suites
\citep{debenedetti2024agentdojo,greshake2023not}, harmful-agent-behaviour
benchmarks \citep{andriushchenko2025agentharm}, and general agent capability
benchmarks \citep{liu2024agentbench,mialon2024gaia}.

The gap this paper addresses is that safety interventions in agentic systems are
almost always exogenous to the model's internals---system prompts, filters,
guardrail classifiers, or critic passes
\citep{inan2023llamaguard,rebedea2023nemoguardrails}---while activation-level
interventions are almost always evaluated on monolithic single-turn chat
\citep{rimsky2024steering,arditi2024refusal}. Where an agentic pipeline exposes
distinct roles (here, a planner and a critic), the \emph{location} of an
activation intervention is itself a design variable, and we treat it as one to be
measured rather than assumed. Our primary comparisons target the planner role;
the archived material that varies the role label (planner-only, critic-only,
both) does so across a run-phase boundary and at a single strength, so
configuration is confounded with phase and strength, and we report it as
associational and exploratory only. We also include an exploratory prompt-filter
baseline (Addition~6) precisely because a guardrail-style intervention
\citep{inan2023llamaguard} is the relevant point of comparison for deployment;
that comparison, however, has asymmetric missing-label counts across conditions,
a non-comparable outcome definition, no test and no correction, and no recorded
model identifier, so it establishes nothing about the relative merits of the two
intervention classes. What remains open is a clean, same-session, same-outcome
factorial over intervention location, strength, and vector identity within one
pipeline.

\subsection{Multilingual safety and evaluation}

Multilingual safety findings have consistently shown that alignment does not
transfer uniformly across languages. Low-resource-language translation is an
effective jailbreak channel \citep{yong2024lowresource}; multilingual prompts
raise unsafe-response rates relative to English \citep{deng2024multilingual};
safety behaviour varies widely across languages on parallel test suites
\citep{wang2024all}; and safety-relevant failures track a broader ``language
barrier'' in instruction following \citep{shen2024language}. Structural causes
are well documented: resource asymmetry across the world's languages
\citep{joshi2020state}, tokenisation and cost disparities
\citep{ahia2023languages}, scarce evaluation and instruction data for African
languages
\citep{adelani2021masakhaner,adelani2022masakhaner2,adelani2024irokobench,ogundepo2023afriqa,nekoto2020participatory},
partly addressed by multilingual instruction-tuning efforts
\citep{ustun2024aya,singh2024aya}, and evidence that multilingual models route
through anglocentric latent representations with partly language-specific
components \citep{wendler2024llamas,tang2024languagespecific}. The over-refusal
side of the trade-off is measured mostly in English
\citep{rottger2024xstest,cui2024orbench}.

Two gaps follow. First, this literature is dominated by descriptive
baselining---attack success or refusal rates per language
\citep{wang2024all,deng2024multilingual}---rather than paired, per-language tests
of an \emph{intervention}. Second, the utility side is rarely measured in the
same languages as the harm side \citep{rottger2024xstest}. This paper evaluates
planner-targeted steering per language with matched prompts and a per-language
inferential decision, in Arabic, English, Hausa, and Yoruba, and finds
model-dependent and language-dependent directions of effect, including a
significant \emph{worsening} in one language of one model. We keep the benign
(utility) arm in a separate correction family from the harmful arm, and we do
not read a null benign result as evidence of no utility cost: baseline refusal
sits at the floor and the Yoruba benign cell contributes only 45 paired prompts,
so power there is minimal. Our exploratory Hindi/Korean/Swahili material is
descriptive, uses a different estimand (harmful veto rate), and has undocumented
malformed-output counts, so it cannot be pooled with the main languages. Closed
models enter only as descriptive context: a single-condition frontier baseline
and a prompt-matched cross-version comparison. The latter is a reminder,
consistent with reports of behavioural drift in commercial APIs
\citep{chen2023chatgptbehavior}, that closed-baseline comparisons inherit
uncontrolled version variation; no vendor snapshot is recorded for any model
entry here, which limits temporal generalisation for all of them.

\subsection{Evaluation methodology and correction families}

Our statistical apparatus is standard in isolation---the exact paired McNemar
test for matched binary outcomes \citep{mcnemar1947note}, paired nonparametric
bootstrap percentile intervals \citep{efron1993introduction}, Holm's
sequentially rejective correction \citep{holm1979simple} as an alternative to
false-discovery control \citep{benjamini1995controlling}, and Cohen's $\kappa$
for annotator agreement \citep{cohen1960coefficient}---and the methodological
literature on how to use it in NLP is mature: significance-testing practice
\citep{dror2018hitchhiker}, replicability across datasets
\citep{dror2017replicability}, statistical power \citep{card2020little},
variance accounting in benchmarks \citep{bouthillier2021accounting}, holistic
multi-metric reporting \citep{liang2023holistic}, and the reliability limits of
model-based judges \citep{zheng2023judging}. Safety-specific evaluation suites
have likewise standardised attack sets and metrics
\citep{zou2023universal,mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,shen2024donowanything}.

In practice, however, steering papers rarely state a correction family at all,
frequently pool cells across models, languages, and outcomes, and seldom
distinguish an interval excluding zero from a family-corrected rejection. The
methodological contribution here is procedural rather than technical. (i) Each
model entry carries its own \emph{disjoint}, pre-declared Holm family
\citep{holm1979simple}---Llama harmful (4), Llama benign (4), Qwen (33 steered
cells), Gemma (3), GPT-OSS (4)---and these are never merged, pooled, or
averaged; every significance statement is internal to one model, one outcome,
and one family, and we therefore report no family-corrected cross-model
contrast. (ii) Where an interval excludes zero but Holm does not reject, both
are reported jointly and the cell is described as non-significant; the cells
where the two diverge are enumerated rather than summarised, in the spirit of
\citet{dror2018hitchhiker}. (iii) Partial coverage is reported at exact recorded
denominators (e.g.\ steered $n=6$, paired $n=160$ and $n=173$ in the Qwen
family; 101 paired English benign records for Gemma; harmful denominators of
286--291 for the GPT-5 baseline) rather than rounded to nominal targets, and
per-model totals are not synthesised where none is recorded---a precaution
motivated by the power and variance literature
\citep{card2020little,bouthillier2021accounting}. (iv) Missing labels are
handled explicitly in the sense demanded by the missing-data literature
\citep{rubin1976inference,little2019statistical}: complete-case policies are
stated, no outcome is imputed, a duplicate record subset is not pooled with the
analysed set, and---critically---where the decision-semantics and parser audits
that define an outcome are not available, we state that the outcome composition
is declared rather than independently verified and report no parse-failure or
missing-label counts for that model. Our exploratory additions engage the
methodological literature in the same spirit: the activation-patching audit
(Addition~1) has exactly one evaluable row under its own recovery definition and
is reported as such, in line with warnings that patching conclusions depend
sharply on metric and denominator choices
\citep{meng2022locating,vig2020investigating,zhang2024towards,heimersheim2024use};
the judge-calibration study (Addition~4) reports $\kappa$ in $\kappa$ units
\citep{cohen1960coefficient} but notes that annotators were not blinded to the
original labels and that 98 prompts cannot support generalisation, which is
precisely the regime in which model-based judging is least reliable
\citep{zheng2023judging}; and the adversarial check (Addition~5) records zero
jailbreak events in both conditions on 50 English prompts with a random vector,
so no test is defined and the check has no power. None of Additions~1--6
declares a correction family or is treated as confirmatory.

\subsection{Positioning and what remains open}

Taken together, prior work establishes that activation steering can change model
behaviour (mostly in English, mostly single-model, sometimes with mechanistic
evidence) \citep{turner2023activation,rimsky2024steering,arditi2024refusal},
that agentic pipelines expose role structure that safety interventions could
target (mostly evaluated with exogenous guardrails)
\citep{yao2023react,ruan2024toolemu,andriushchenko2025agentharm}, and that
multilingual safety is unevenly distributed (mostly documented descriptively)
\citep{yong2024lowresource,deng2024multilingual,wang2024all}. This paper
contributes the intersection that is missing: a multilingual (four languages),
multi-model (four open-weight entries plus two closed descriptive baselines),
agentic evaluation of \emph{planner-targeted} steering, with disjoint per-model
correction families \citep{holm1979simple}, harmful and benign arms held
separate, exact partial-coverage denominators, explicit missing-label provenance
\citep{rubin1976inference}, and a set of clearly labelled exploratory additions.
It does not contribute mechanism, causal identification, or cross-model
inference. Four gaps remain open and are, in our view, the natural next targets:
a same-session matched random-vector control to test vector specificity, whose
absence is exactly the failure mode flagged by
\citet{tan2024analysing} and \citet{makelov2024illusion}; a within-phase
factorial over intervention role and strength so that role is not confounded
with run phase; adequately powered benign arms in low-resource languages, where
our own design is at the floor \citep{rottger2024xstest,card2020little}; and
shipped decision-semantics and parser audits for every model entry, without
which outcome definitions remain declared rather than verifiable.
