\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Steering and activation engineering}

Activation-level control of language models has developed from a small set of
recipes: extracting latent steering vectors by optimisation
(Subramani et al., 2022), adding contrastive activation differences at inference
time (Turner et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024), intervening on
probe-identified directions to change truthfulness behaviour
(Li et al., 2023), and reading and controlling higher-level concepts through
representation engineering (Zou et al., 2023). A parallel line treats weight-
and task-space edits as the control surface (Ilharco et al., 2023), and a third
identifies interpretable directions with sparse autoencoders
(Cunningham et al., 2023; Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024). Most
directly relevant to safety, Arditi et al.\ (2024) report that refusal in
several open-weight chat models is well described by a single mediating
direction, and Marks and Tegmark (2024) and von R\"utte et al.\ (2024) study
the geometry of related concept directions.

Three features of this literature bound its evidential reach, and they define
the space in which the present paper sits. First, evaluation is overwhelmingly
English and single-turn: steering effects are measured on chat completions or
instruction prompts, not on decisions taken inside a running system. Second,
steering vectors generalise unevenly across prompt distributions and models;
Tan et al.\ (2024) show that reliability varies substantially by behaviour and
setting, which makes single-model demonstrations weak evidence for the technique
as such. Third, mechanistic and behavioural claims are often reported together
even when only the latter is measured. We deliberately stay on the behavioural
side. This paper reports prompt-matched, within-model condition contrasts, and
makes \emph{no} mechanistic or localisation claim: it does not replicate,
confirm, or extend the refusal-direction results above, the historical steering
direction used in the archived Phase~2 material is not available in our evidence
base, and no matched random-vector control on the same prompts and outcome
definition exists anywhere in it. Generic perturbation is therefore neither
identified nor ruled out, and vector specificity is not established here. What
is new relative to this literature is scope rather than mechanism: a
multilingual, multi-model, agentic-pipeline evaluation of steering applied at a
specific pipeline \emph{role}, with per-model correction families and explicit
coverage accounting.

\subsection{Agentic systems and tool use}

Agentic LLM systems decompose a task across roles and tool calls: interleaved
reasoning and acting (Yao et al., 2023), tool invocation
(Schick et al., 2023), self-critique and revision
(Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023), model-written critiques as an
alignment mechanism (Bai et al., 2022), multi-agent debate
(Du et al., 2023), and orchestration frameworks with explicit role assignment
(Park et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024). Safety evaluation for
such systems has grown correspondingly: sandboxed risk elicitation
(Ruan et al., 2024), prompt-injection and tool-abuse suites
(Debenedetti et al., 2024), harmful-agent-behaviour benchmarks
(Andriushchenko et al., 2025), and general agent capability benchmarks
(Liu et al., 2024; Mialon et al., 2024).

The gap this paper addresses is that safety interventions in agentic systems are
almost always exogenous to the model's internals---system prompts, filters,
guardrail classifiers, or critic passes---while activation-level interventions
are almost always evaluated on monolithic single-turn chat. Where an agentic
pipeline exposes distinct roles (here, a planner and a critic), the
\emph{location} of an activation intervention is itself a design variable, and
we treat it as one to be measured rather than assumed. Our primary comparisons
target the planner role; the archived material that varies the role label
(planner-only, critic-only, both) does so across a run-phase boundary and at a
single strength, so configuration is confounded with phase and strength, and we
report it as associational and exploratory only. We also include an exploratory
prompt-filter baseline (Addition~6) precisely because a guardrail-style
intervention is the relevant point of comparison for deployment; that
comparison, however, has asymmetric missing-label counts across conditions, a
non-comparable outcome definition, no test and no correction, and no recorded
model identifier, so it establishes nothing about the relative merits of the two
intervention classes. What remains open is a clean, same-session, same-outcome
factorial over intervention location, strength, and vector identity within one
pipeline.

\subsection{Multilingual safety and evaluation}

Multilingual safety findings have consistently shown that alignment does not
transfer uniformly across languages. Low-resource-language translation is an
effective jailbreak channel (Yong et al., 2024); multilingual prompts raise
unsafe-response rates relative to English (Deng et al., 2024); safety behaviour
varies widely across languages on parallel test suites
(Wang et al., 2024, XSafety); and safety-relevant failures track a broader
``language barrier'' in instruction following (Shen et al., 2024). Structural
causes are well documented: resource asymmetry across the world's languages
(Joshi et al., 2020), tokenisation and cost disparities
(Ahia et al., 2023), scarce evaluation data for African languages
(Adelani et al., 2021; Adelani et al., 2024), and evidence that multilingual
models route through anglocentric latent representations with partly
language-specific components (Wendler et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024). The
over-refusal side of the trade-off is measured mostly in English
(R\"ottger et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2024).

Two gaps follow. First, this literature is dominated by descriptive
baselining---attack success or refusal rates per language---rather than paired,
per-language tests of an \emph{intervention}. Second, the utility side is rarely
measured in the same languages as the harm side. This paper evaluates
planner-targeted steering per language with matched prompts and a per-language
inferential decision, in Arabic, English, Hausa, and Yoruba, and finds
model-dependent and language-dependent directions of effect, including a
significant \emph{worsening} in one language of one model. We keep the benign
(utility) arm in a separate correction family from the harmful arm, and we do
not read a null benign result as evidence of no utility cost: baseline refusal
sits at the floor and the Yoruba benign cell contributes only 45 paired prompts,
so power there is minimal. Our exploratory Hindi/Korean/Swahili material is
descriptive, uses a different estimand (harmful veto rate), and has undocumented
malformed-output counts, so it cannot be pooled with the main languages. Closed
models enter only as descriptive context: a single-condition frontier baseline
and a prompt-matched cross-version comparison. The latter is a reminder,
consistent with reports of behavioural drift in commercial APIs
(Chen et al., 2023), that closed-baseline comparisons inherit uncontrolled
version variation; no vendor snapshot is recorded for any model entry here,
which limits temporal generalisation for all of them.

\subsection{Evaluation methodology and correction families}

Our statistical apparatus is standard in isolation---the exact paired McNemar
test for matched binary outcomes (McNemar, 1947), paired nonparametric bootstrap
percentile intervals (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993), Holm's sequentially rejective
correction (Holm, 1979) as an alternative to false-discovery control
(Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), and Cohen's $\kappa$ for annotator agreement
(Cohen, 1960)---and the methodological literature on how to use it in NLP is
mature: significance-testing practice (Dror et al., 2018), replicability across
datasets (Dror et al., 2017), statistical power (Card et al., 2020), variance
accounting in benchmarks (Bouthillier et al., 2021), holistic multi-metric
reporting (Liang et al., 2023), and the reliability limits of model-based judges
(Zheng et al., 2023). Safety-specific evaluation suites have likewise
standardised attack sets and metrics (Zou et al., 2023, GCG;
Mazeika et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024, DAN prompts).

In practice, however, steering papers rarely state a correction family at all,
frequently pool cells across models, languages, and outcomes, and seldom
distinguish an interval excluding zero from a family-corrected rejection. The
methodological contribution here is procedural rather than technical. (i) Each
model entry carries its own \emph{disjoint}, pre-declared Holm family---Llama
harmful (4), Llama benign (4), Qwen (33 steered cells), Gemma (3), GPT-OSS
(4)---and these are never merged, pooled, or averaged; every significance
statement is internal to one model, one outcome, and one family, and we
therefore report no family-corrected cross-model contrast. (ii) Where an
interval excludes zero but Holm does not reject, both are reported jointly and
the cell is described as non-significant; the cells where the two diverge are
enumerated rather than summarised. (iii) Partial coverage is reported at exact
recorded denominators (e.g.\ steered $n=6$, paired $n=160$ and $n=173$ in the
Qwen family; 101 paired English benign records for Gemma; harmful denominators
of 286--291 for the GPT-5 baseline) rather than rounded to nominal targets, and
per-model totals are not synthesised where none is recorded. (iv) Missing labels
are handled explicitly in the sense demanded by the missing-data literature
(Rubin, 1976; Little and Rubin, 2019): complete-case policies are stated, no
outcome is imputed, a duplicate record subset is not pooled with the analysed
set, and---critically---where the decision-semantics and parser audits that
define an outcome are not available, we state that the outcome composition is
declared rather than independently verified and report no parse-failure or
missing-label counts for that model. Our exploratory additions engage the
methodological literature in the same spirit: the activation-patching audit
(Addition~1) has exactly one evaluable row under its own recovery definition and
is reported as such, in line with warnings that patching conclusions depend
sharply on metric and denominator choices
(Meng et al., 2022; Vig et al., 2020; Zhang and Nanda, 2024;
Heimersheim and Nanda, 2024); the judge-calibration study (Addition~4) reports
$\kappa$ in $\kappa$ units but notes that annotators were not blinded to the
original labels and that 98 prompts cannot support generalisation; and the
adversarial check (Addition~5) records zero jailbreak events in both conditions
on 50 English prompts with a random vector, so no test is defined and the check
has no power. None of Additions~1--6 declares a correction family or is treated
as confirmatory.

\subsection{Positioning and what remains open}

Taken together, prior work establishes that activation steering can change model
behaviour (mostly in English, mostly single-model, sometimes with mechanistic
evidence), that agentic pipelines expose role structure that safety
interventions could target (mostly evaluated with exogenous guardrails), and
that multilingual safety is unevenly distributed (mostly documented
descriptively). This paper contributes the intersection that is missing: a
multilingual (four languages), multi-model (four open-weight entries plus two
closed descriptive baselines), agentic evaluation of \emph{planner-targeted}
steering, with disjoint per-model correction families, harmful and benign arms
held separate, exact partial-coverage denominators, explicit missing-label
provenance, and a set of clearly labelled exploratory additions. It does not
contribute mechanism, causal identification, or cross-model inference. Four
gaps remain open and are, in our view, the natural next targets: a same-session
matched random-vector control to test vector specificity; a within-phase
factorial over intervention role and strength so that role is not confounded
with run phase; adequately powered benign arms in low-resource languages, where
our own design is at the floor; and shipped decision-semantics and parser audits
for every model entry, without which outcome definitions remain declared rather
than verifiable.
