\section{Introduction}

Safety behaviour in language models is usually evaluated as a property of a
single model answering a single English prompt. Deployed systems increasingly
depart from both assumptions. Agentic pipelines decompose a task across roles---a
planner that proposes actions, a critic that reviews them, tools that execute
them---so that the decision which matters for safety is taken inside a running
system rather than in a one-shot chat completion. At the same time, these systems
are used multilingually, and multilingual safety is known to be unevenly
distributed: alignment does not transfer uniformly across languages, and
lower-resource languages are both less well covered by evaluation data and more
exposed to failure. Activation steering is an attractive candidate intervention
in this setting precisely because it is endogenous to the model and can be
applied at a chosen point in the pipeline: where a pipeline exposes distinct
roles, the \emph{location} of the intervention becomes a design variable rather
than an implementation detail. Whether steering at a particular role changes
multilingual decision outcomes---and in which direction---is an empirical
question that has largely not been asked.

The existing evidence base is not well positioned to answer it. Steering and
refusal-direction work is overwhelmingly English-only, single-turn, and often
single-model, which makes a favourable demonstration weak evidence for the
technique as such; reliability is known to vary substantially across behaviours
and prompt distributions. Agentic safety evaluation, conversely, is dominated by
exogenous interventions---system prompts, filters, guardrail classifiers, critic
passes---rather than activation-level ones. Multilingual safety work is largely
descriptive baselining of attack success or refusal rates per language, rather
than paired per-language tests of an intervention, and the utility (over-refusal)
side is rarely measured in the same languages as the harm side. Finally,
reporting practice in this area is loose in ways that matter for safety claims:
correction families are frequently unstated, cells are pooled across models,
languages, and outcomes, partial coverage is rounded to nominal denominators, and
an interval excluding zero is often not distinguished from a family-corrected
rejection.

We report a multilingual, multi-model evaluation of planner-targeted activation
steering on a shared prompt set in Arabic, English, Hausa, and Yoruba. Four
open-weight model entries carry the primary comparisons
(\texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct}, Qwen~2.5~72B, \texttt{gemma\_4\_31b},
and \texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b}), each as a prompt-matched, within-model contrast
between an unsteered baseline and a planner-only steered condition, tested with
the exact paired McNemar test and corrected with Holm inside a \emph{pre-declared
and disjoint} family per model entry and outcome: Llama harmful (size~4), Llama
benign (size~4), the Qwen 33-cell role$\times$strength family, the Gemma
three-cell complete-case family, and the GPT-OSS four-comparison harmful family.
These families are never merged, pooled, or averaged, so every significance
statement is internal to one model, one outcome, and one family, and we report no
family-corrected cross-model contrast. Two closed models enter only as
descriptive context: a single-condition frontier baseline
(\texttt{openai/gpt-5}, no intervention arm) and a prompt-matched cross-version
comparison of Claude Sonnet~4 against Sonnet~5, neither of which carries a test
or a correction family. Throughout, partial coverage is reported at exact
recorded denominators rather than nominal targets, harmful and benign arms are
kept in separate families, and missing-label provenance is stated explicitly,
including where the decision-semantics and parser audits that define an outcome
are unavailable and the outcome label is therefore declared rather than
independently verified. Six clearly labelled exploratory additions
(Additions~1--6: an activation-patching endpoint audit, retrospective role
material, a Hindi/Korean/Swahili descriptive replication, judge calibration, an
adversarial robustness check, and a prompt-filter baseline) are reported
separately, declare no correction family, and are treated as
hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The resulting picture is conditional rather than uniform. For Llama, harmful
operational failure is substantially lower under planner-only steering in
Arabic, Hausa, and Yoruba, with all three reductions surviving Holm correction
inside the harmful family, while English---the language with the lowest baseline
failure rate---is essentially unchanged. For Qwen, the Holm-significant cells are
harmful cells in Arabic and English, with the largest observed reduction in
English harmful; the Hausa and Yoruba harmful point reductions do not survive
correction, and no benign cell is significant at any role or strength. GPT-OSS is
mixed in direction: English improved significantly, Hausa \emph{worsened}
significantly, Arabic showed a point reduction that did not survive Holm
correction, and Yoruba showed no marginal change despite prompt-level
disagreements in both directions. In the three Gemma cells for which paired
records exist, all point estimates move toward more non-veto failure under
steering, but no cell is Holm-significant. On the utility side, the Llama benign
refusal family shows no Holm-significant change with refusal at or near the floor
in every language; because that arm is small and floor-valued, we read this as
consistent with, but not establishing, an absence of utility cost. The two
closed-model analyses are descriptive only, and the cross-version comparison
records that outcome-relevant behaviour can shift across closed-model versions on
identical prompts---background variation that any evaluation conducted against
closed baselines inherits. None of these comparisons is causal or mechanistic:
they contrast conditions on matched prompts within single model entries and
license no claim about why outcomes moved.

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A multilingual, multi-model evaluation of role-targeted
    steering.} We evaluate planner-targeted activation steering across four
    open-weight model entries and four languages (Arabic, English, Hausa,
    Yoruba), with harmful and benign arms measured in the same languages, plus
    two closed models as descriptive baselines---an intersection of scope
    (multilingual $\times$ multi-model $\times$ agentic role) that prior
    steering work does not cover.
  \item \textbf{A reporting protocol built for safety claims.} Each model entry
    and outcome carries its own pre-declared, disjoint Holm family; harmful and
    benign arms are never combined; intervals that exclude zero without a Holm
    rejection are reported jointly and described as non-significant, with the
    diverging cells enumerated; partial coverage is reported at exact recorded
    denominators; and missing-label handling and outcome-composition provenance
    are stated per model, including where they are unavailable.
  \item \textbf{A model- and language-dependent empirical result, including a
    negative direction.} We show that the same nominal intervention type is
    associated with large family-corrected reductions in harmful operational
    failure in some model$\times$language cells, with no detectable movement
    where baseline failure is already low, and with a significant
    \emph{worsening} in one language of one model---so that per-model,
    per-language measurement with its own correction family, rather than
    extrapolation from a single favourable cell, is required.
  \item \textbf{An explicit account of what the evidence cannot support.} We
    report six exploratory additions without correction families or confirmatory
    tests, and we state the gaps they leave open: no matched random-vector
    control on the same prompts and outcome definition exists, so generic
    perturbation is neither identified nor ruled out and vector specificity is
    not established; role varies jointly with run phase and strength in the
    archived cross-phase material; and benign arms in low-resource languages are
    at the floor and underpowered.
\end{itemize}
