\section{Conclusion}

Planner-targeted activation steering changed multilingual decision outcomes in
the open-weight model entries we evaluated, but the changes were large,
uneven, and not uniformly favourable. Within their own pre-declared correction
families, Llama~3.3~70B showed Holm-significant reductions in harmful
operational failure in Arabic, Hausa, and Yoruba with English essentially
unchanged, and Qwen~2.5~72B showed Holm-significant reductions confined to the
Arabic and English harmful cells, with the Hausa and Yoruba point reductions not
surviving correction inside the 33-cell family. GPT-OSS~20B was
mixed in direction rather than uniformly beneficial: English improved
significantly, Hausa \emph{worsened} significantly, Arabic did not survive Holm
correction, and Yoruba showed no marginal change. All available Gemma cells moved
toward more non-veto failure in point estimates without any family-corrected
rejection, and the Llama benign refusal family was null at or near the floor in
every language---consistent with, but not establishing, an absence of utility
cost. The closed-model analyses are purely descriptive: GPT-5 is a
single-condition frontier baseline with no intervention arm, and Claude
Sonnet~4 vs~5 is a prompt-matched cross-version record carrying no test,
interval, or correction family. Because every family is disjoint and never
pooled, no statement here is a cross-model estimate, and none of the comparisons
is causal or mechanistic.

The practical implication is that multilingual steering claims should be
evaluated the way we have tried to report them: pre-registered, per-model and
per-language, with disjoint correction families, a benign-side outcome measured
alongside the harmful-side one, and exact recorded denominators rather than
nominal targets. A favourable result in one language or one model entry provides
no warrant for another, and heterogeneity across languages---especially
lower-resource ones---is the phenomenon to be measured rather than averaged
away. Four directions follow directly from the gaps identified above. First,
same-session matched random-vector controls on the same prompts and outcome
definition, which no artifact in our evidence base provides, are required before
any claim of vector specificity can be made. Second, cross-version and
cross-snapshot audits are needed, since dated vendor snapshots are unrecorded
here and closed-model behaviour on identical prompts demonstrably shifts across
versions. Third, language coverage should be expanded beyond the four primary
languages, with sufficient benign-arm power to detect small increases in false
refusals. Fourth, deployment-focused robustness checks---multilingual
adversarial sets with non-zero event rates, shipped decision-semantics and
parser audits, and complete missing-label accounting---are needed before
planner-targeted steering is treated as a safety mechanism rather than an
empirical design variable under study.
